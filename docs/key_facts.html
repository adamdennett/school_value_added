Key facts: schools in Brighton and Hove

Eight things the published data establish about the city’s school system, in order of how much they should change a decision

Published

November 24, 2026

NoteAbout this page
  • What it is: a briefing. Every claim here is drawn from the analyses on this site, from How to Pull the Right Lever and from the Brighton and Hove school system model. Each fact links to the page that computes it, so anything here can be checked.
  • Data: published data only — DfE, Ofsted and ONS. No pupil-level record is used anywhere.
  • Written to be argued with. Where the evidence cuts against the way the city’s schools are usually described — in either direction — that is said plainly. Section 10 lists what would change these conclusions.
  • Vintage: the figures are as at the November 2026 build. Value added covers 2021/22 to 2024/25; absence is full-year 2024/25 unless stated.

If you have five minutes

  1. Brighton and Hove is a national success story on attainment — On value-added, it’s 6th of 152 local authorities (all children) — and almost all of that is the city’s schools holding up results against some of the highest absence in England (3rd of 152 for secondaries in 2024/25).
  2. Absence is the biggest single issue for schools in the city, and it is not poverty. Disadvantage here is average; secondary absence was the 3rd highest in England in 2024/25. Other LEAs with deeper disadvantage have better attendance.
  3. In raw-terms (with nothing allowed for) its disadvantaged children leave school with Attainment 8 ranked 122nd of 152. Bring absence alone to England’s average and the city moves to about 69th; allow for intake and context as well and its authority effect for those pupils is 18th of 151. All of these are true at once, and the gap between them is mostly absence.
  4. Our perception of a ‘good school’ is wrong. Attainment 8 is not a measure of a good school - it’s mainly a measure of who goes there. But is is what parents in Brighton select on Nationally it correlates with what a school actually adds at 0.27, and intake alone predicts 81% of it. The city needs to change its messaging, publish and lead on a value-added measure (I have developed one), particularly to change perceptions of schools like BACA.
  5. Brighton has a problem with accessibility. Children in the city’s poorest neighbourhoods live further from a school than their better-off neighbours — almost uniquely in England. It traces back to the closure of CoMArt in 2005.
  6. The looming demographic crisis is locked in. Year 7 falls by a fifth by 2032/33, and those children are already in the city’s primary schools. Longhill has about 3.7 years of reserve. My new simulator can help us think of solutions.
  7. Decline in children is driven by low fertility, not house prices keeping people out. 0.98 children per woman in 2025 against England’s 1.39 — about 70% of the national rate — and over 2023–25 the joint second-lowest of 292 comparable districts, level with Westminster and behind only Cambridge. Families do also leave: the city is 31st of 297 districts on net child migration, while neighbouring Lewes, Mid Sussex and Wealden all gain children at much the same house prices. At Year 7 it is about half the recent fall.
  8. Social mixing is the weakest lever for narrowing the attainment gap the council has - and with additional travel could make it worse. Attendance and fixing easy access to schools in the east will improve attainment and address the double disadvantage in the city.
  9. None of this needs new data collection. I’ve been building tools on open data to assist with policy scenario testing - biggest barrier is awareness and open-mindedness.
ImportantThe thread running through all of it

The city is arguing about school quality using a number that is four-fifths intake. Attainment 8 tells you who a school admits; value added tells you what it does with them. Nationally the two correlate at 0.27, and in Brighton and Hove at 0.31 — eight of the ten schools move four places or more between the two tables.

Almost every mistake on this page follows from conflating the two. It is why the city reads itself as 122nd when its schools are 18th (section 2). It is why the school doing most for disadvantaged children sits last in the published table and half empty (section 3). It is why social mixing looks like the lever and attendance does not (section 8). And it is the one thing here the council could change at no cost, with no consultation, by next admissions round: publish a value-added measure beside Attainment 8, and point families at it.

1. The city is a national success story on value added

Value Added v2.1 measures every state secondary in England against what its intake and context predict: prior attainment, disadvantage, special educational needs, pupil mobility, English as an additional language, school structure, neighbourhood education and absence.

  • The city’s ten secondaries average +1.4 Attainment 8 points against England. Five are significantly above expectation — their 95% intervals sit clear of zero — and none is significantly below. Two do score below expectation (Longhill -1.5, Patcham -2.0), but both intervals still cross zero, Patcham’s only barely (-4.0 to +0.1), so neither is distinguishable from the schools it is being compared with (Brighton and Hove).
  • The local authority’s own effect is +3.7% of Attainment 8, the 6th largest of 152 authorities. It is +3.9% for disadvantaged pupils (18th of 151), +3.7% for non-disadvantaged (4th), +2.8% for girls (14th) and +4.5% for boys (4th). It is there for every group and in every year (geographical effects).
  • It is an absence effect, and it almost exactly cancels one. Over the four fitted years the city’s schools ran 10.9% absence against England’s 9.0%. On the model’s absence coefficient that difference lowers expected Attainment 8 by 4.5%. The authority effect, +3.7%, gives nearly all of it back.
ImportantThe honest version of this fact

The right sentence is “the city’s schools achieve about what similar intakes achieve elsewhere in England, despite unusually high absence” — not “the city’s schools add more than others”. Take absence out of the model and the authority falls from 6th to 59th, an effect indistinguishable from zero. On value added computed with no allowance for absence at all, the city’s schools average -0.7 points, 102nd of 151 authorities.

That is still a real and unusual finding. It is not a licence to stop worrying.

The “Brighton effect” is partly how educated the city is

  • Around the city’s schools, 43.4% of adults hold a degree-level (level 4+) qualification, against 33.2% around England’s schools — 25th of 152 authorities, and 9 of the 10 schools sit above England’s median school (geographical effects).
  • Across the city as a whole, 44.5% of adults aged 16 and over hold a degree-level qualification against England’s 33.8% (Census 2021, TS067). Among South East cities that is second only to Oxford (48.1%), ahead of Bristol (42.1%), Reading (40.4%), Canterbury (33.1%), Southampton (31.6%) and Portsmouth (30.0%). Counting every South East district, including the affluent commuter districts, the city is 8th of 64 — behind Elmbridge, Oxford, Waverley, Wokingham, Windsor and Maidenhead, Winchester and Woking.
  • The model already credits this, and cannot credit more. Neighbourhood education lifts the city’s expected Attainment 8 by about +1.1 points for all pupils, +1.3 for non-disadvantaged and only +0.3 for disadvantaged pupils. It accounts for about 32% of what would otherwise look like a city effect. Drawing the neighbourhood more tightly (the 5, 10, 25, 50 or 100 nearest output areas, or distance-weighted) fits worse at every step and leaves the effect larger. The best-fitting version moves the city’s effect only from +3.7% to +3.6%.
  • What the Census cannot see is the likeliest explanation of the rest. The measure counts every adult, not parents — students, whose highest qualification while studying is usually level 3, and young graduates with no school-age children both sit in it, pulling opposite ways. The leading reading of the leftover is family resources that keep learning going when a child misses a day: a parent who can help catch up, flexible or home-based work, tutoring, books. Free school meals does not see this, and neither, quite, does a Census count of all adults.

2. Its disadvantaged children still leave with poor results

This is the fact that should sit beside the one above, and it is the one most often missing from the city’s account of itself.

What 122nd is, and what it is not

Every figure in the table above is raw. It is the Attainment 8 the city’s disadvantaged pupils actually achieved, with no allowance for absence, intake or anything else. That is the right number for asking how are these children doing? — and the wrong one for asking how are these schools doing? The two questions have different answers, and the distance between them is mostly absence.

What is allowed for Disadvantaged pupils’ standing
Nothing — raw Attainment 8 33.9 points, 122nd of 152
Absence only, brought to England’s average 36.9 points, 69th
Intake, context and absence — the authority effect on v2.1 +3.9%, 18th of 151
the same model with the absence term taken out -0.0%, 78th of 151

Read the middle row first. If every Brighton and Hove school’s absence fell to England’s school average of 8.6% in 2024/25 and nothing else changed, the model puts its disadvantaged pupils’ Attainment 8 up by 3.0 points on average — and the city moves from 122nd to about 69th. The gain is concentrated where absence is worst: +6.7 points at Brighton Aldridge, +6.2 at Longhill, +5.3 at Hove Park, and nothing at all at King’s and Patcham, which already run at or below the national rate.

So absence is most of the shortfall, but not all of it. Even with absence at England’s average the city would sit at 36.9 against England’s 38.0 — still below, but a little over half a point rather than four points. What is left is the region, the disadvantage gap itself, and special educational needs.

The bottom two rows are the same point from the other end. Once intake and context and absence are allowed for, the city’s authority effect for disadvantaged pupils is +3.9%, 18th of 151. Strip just the absence term out of that model and it collapses to -0.0%, 78th. Absence is what separates 18th from 78th.

ImportantHow to say this without overclaiming

“Our disadvantaged children are 122nd of 152 on raw results, and on the model’s arithmetic absence accounts for most of that gap.” Not “we would be 69th if absence were fixed” as a prediction.

Three reasons. The middle row holds every other authority at today’s absence, so it is “if Brighton alone closed its absence gap”, not a league table anyone will ever see. The absence gradient is associational: it says what schools with less absence look like once intake is accounted for, not what these schools would gain by cutting absence by a given amount. And part of the gradient is standing in for things absence travels with — family stress, poverty beyond free school meals, disengagement — so some of that 3.0 points would not arrive just because attendance improved.

What survives all three caveats is the direction and the order of magnitude: on every way of cutting it, absence is the largest single thing standing between the city’s disadvantaged children and the results their intake predicts.

The level moves a long way. The gap moves less

Worth separating, because the national target is written in terms of the gap and these two answers differ.

Disadvantaged pupils’ Attainment 8 33.9 36.9 +3.0
Non-disadvantaged pupils’ Attainment 8 49.6 51.9 +2.3
The disadvantage gap 15.7 15.0 -0.7
Rank on the gap (1 = widest) 13th 22nd
  • The level is transformed: 122nd to 69th. That is the number that matters for a child, and it is the case for treating absence as the city’s first-order problem.
  • The gap narrows by only 0.7 points, because better attendance lifts everyone. It closes about 19% of the 3.6 points by which the city’s gap exceeds England’s — real, but not the whole story.
  • Absence is still the most progressive lever available. The gradient is 77% steeper for disadvantaged pupils, so they gain 3.0 points against 2.3. Almost nothing else in the model helps them more than everyone else; neighbourhood education, for instance, is worth +2.6% to a non-disadvantaged pupil and +0.8% to a disadvantaged one (section 8).

The honest conclusion, and the better argument. A gap can be closed by the top doing worse, and it tells you nothing about whether any child learned more. The level tells you that. Absence is the largest lever on the level for the city’s poorest children by a wide margin, and a moderately progressive lever on the gap as well. If the choice is between a policy that narrows the gap on paper and one that raises disadvantaged children’s results by three points, take the three points.

  • The gap is the 13th widest in England and three and a half points wider than England’s.
  • The region makes it worse. For disadvantaged pupils the South East is the worst region in England: -4.1% of Attainment 8 against England, while London is +5.1%. London’s lead belongs to its disadvantaged pupils alone — its non-disadvantaged pupils are at -1.5% (geographical effects across England).
  • Place matters most for exactly the group policy most wants to help. One standard deviation of local authority effect is worth 3.8% of Attainment 8 for disadvantaged pupils against 2.4% for non-disadvantaged; a region, 2.7% against 1.6%.

3. Attainment 8 is not a measure of a good school — and the whole debate runs on it

This is the measurement error underneath every other argument in the city, and it is the cheapest thing on this page to fix.

Nationally, the headline score barely tells you what a school adds

  • Raw Attainment 8 and Value Added v2.1 correlate at 0.27 across England’s 3,265 state secondaries. The headline score accounts for about 7% of what a school actually adds.
  • Intake and context alone predict 81% of the variation in a school’s headline Attainment 8. Four-fifths of the number published about a school is a description of the children who walked through the door.
  • The spreads say the same thing. Headline Attainment 8 varies across schools with a standard deviation of 9.3 points; value added, 2.6 points. Most of what a league table displays is not the school.
  • So the headline number actively misleads, in both directions. Of the schools in the top quarter on raw Attainment 8, 38% are below average on value added. Of those in the bottom quarter, 28% are above average. A parent choosing on the published score has close to a coin-flip’s information about whether the school is any good at teaching.

In Brighton and Hove the two tables are almost unrelated

The city’s ten schools, ranked by the number families are pointed at, and by what the schools add. Within the city the two correlate at 0.31.

  • Longhill is 20.7 Attainment 8 points below the city’s highest, and 4.6 points below the best on value added. Most of what separates it from the popular schools is its intake, not its teaching.
  • Brighton Aldridge is last of the ten on the headline score and 5th on value added — while doing more for disadvantaged pupils than any school in the city (section 8).

And it is the same error the city makes about itself

Section 2 is this error at city scale. Brighton and Hove is 122nd of 152 on raw disadvantaged Attainment 8 and 18th of 151 once intake, context and absence are allowed for. Both numbers describe the same schools in the same year. Which one you quote decides whether the city looks like a failure or a success, and only one of them is about the schools.

The loop this creates, and who pays for it

The council writes the admissions guide, and it tells parents to look at each school’s exam results and Ofsted reports. Families do as advised: headline Attainment 8 explains 84% of how heavily each school is preferred, and value added 20%. Each point of headline score goes with about 7% more preferences per place.

That advice is self-defeating, and it runs in one direction only:

a school with a more disadvantaged intake posts a lower headline score → it is chosen less → its roll shrinks and its intake becomes more disadvantaged → its headline score falls again.

The schools the published number punishes are precisely the ones doing most for the city’s poorest children. Brighton Aldridge is the clearest case in England-wide terms, and it is the school the headline table puts last.

What changing the number would be worth

Run the city’s school system model with families reading the four-year value added measure as closely as they now read the headline score — same places, same catchments, same rules, same journeys, only the number families are taken to be reading changes:

  • Brighton Aldridge goes from 90 children to 174 on 180 places: half empty to 97% full. Hove Park and Portslade Aldridge fill.
  • The share of the city’s empty Year 7 places sitting in just three schools falls from 93% to 75% — the difference between a surplus that is a budgeting problem and one that is a viability problem (section 6).
  • Mean journeys shorten, and deprived children’s extra travel falls from 3.5 to 2.6 minutes each way.
  • It creates no places and removes none. What changes is which schools are empty.

It is the largest single change modelled anywhere in this work, it costs nothing, and it needs no consultation — only a different sentence in a guide the council already publishes, and a contextualised measure printed beside the headline one. That measure exists: it is on this site, for every state secondary in England.

ImportantThe one-sentence version

Attainment 8 measures who a school admits; value added measures what it does with them. Parents, councillors and campaigners are arguing about school quality using a number that is four-fifths intake — and the city’s own admissions guide is what points them at it. Publish both, and lead with the one that is about the school.

4. Absence is the biggest lever, and it is not poverty

How bad it is

  • 10.8% overall absence in state-funded secondary schools in 2024/25, against England’s 8.4% — the 3rd highest of 152 local authorities, behind Knowsley (11.0%) and Bradford. Across all state schools, primary and special included, it was 8.0% against 6.8%, 4th of 153 (DfE, Pupil absence in schools in England, full year 2024/25).
  • It had been getting worse. On the same official basis the city’s secondaries were 9th in 2021/22, 21st in 2022/23, 7th in 2023/24 and 3rd in 2024/25.
  • The first two terms of 2025/26 show it easing. Autumn and spring absence was 9.8% against England’s 8.0%, 6th of 152, down from 10.3% and 3rd over the same two terms of 2024/25. The city fell by 0.5 points, England by 0.1. Full-year figures for 2025/26 are due in March 2027 (DfE, Pupil absence in schools in England: autumn and spring terms, school-level file, summed to authorities).
  • Persistent absence 29.4%, 6th of 152 (England 23.4%) in 2024/25; 25.5% and 17th over the first two terms of 2025/26 (England 21.6%).
  • Disadvantaged pupils’ absence 18.0% against England’s 13.2%, 3rd of 150 authorities (DfE, absence by pupil characteristics).
  • Severe absence — missing half of schooling or more — runs 52% above England’s average school, and 11% above even after intake is allowed for.

Why it is not poverty

  • Disadvantage in the city is average: FSM6 28.8% against England’s 28.4%, 74th of 152. Mean prior attainment (KS2) is 50th of 152.
  • Suspensions are low: 13.0 episodes per 100 pupils against England’s 21.2; 5.9 pupils per 100 suspended at least once against 7.3. This is not a discipline problem.
  • It is above what the intake predicts. On the v2.1 intake terms the city’s schools ran +1.7 percentage points above the absence their pupils’ profiles predict — 3rd highest of 152 authorities. Of the 2.7-point gap with England, about 1.7 points is not explained by who lives here.
  • That residual is genuinely unusual, and growing.
    • Against other authorities: 2.2 standard deviations above their average, in the top 2%. Newcastle (+2.2) and Bradford (+1.9) are above it; York and Gateshead (+1.7, +1.6) are level with it.
    • Not chance: a random set of ten English secondaries averages this much above prediction about 1 time in 250.
    • By year: 5th in 2021/22, 7th in 2022/23, 6th in 2023/24 and 1st in 2024/25 (+2.1).
    • What it can’t say: this is absence the model does not explain, not proof of worse practice. Recording practice, health and journeys to school all sit in it.
  • Seven of the ten schools run more than a point above their predicted absence. Longhill is +4.2, Brighton Aldridge +3.7, Blatchington Mill +2.3. Only King’s runs more than a point below.

What kind of absence

Unauthorised 3.2 3.0 +0.3

  • Of the 1.9 points of extra absence, 1.4 is authorised illness. Unauthorised absence is close to England’s.
  • After intake is allowed for, the city’s illness effect is +27%, 2nd of 152 authorities; its unauthorised effect is -5%, 110th (what goes with absence).
  • This is where the interesting question sits. Authorised illness may include anxiety and health-related absence that behaves differently from disengagement; it may also reflect how the city’s schools code and authorise absence. School-level data cannot separate those two, and the geographical effects page lists the published tables that would.

What it costs, and what it is worth

  • The average city secondary’s absence (11.3%, each school counted equally) against the average English secondary’s (8.6%) is worth about -2.8 Attainment 8 points for all pupils, -3.2 for disadvantaged pupils and -2.4 for non-disadvantaged. The gradient is 77% steeper for disadvantaged pupils.
  • Cutting a school’s absence by a tenth goes with Attainment 8 2.5% higher for all pupils, 3.5% for disadvantaged pupils and 1.9% for non-disadvantaged. Absence is the only large lever in the model that narrows the gap (the simulator).
  • Indicatively, if each school’s absence fell to England’s 8.6%: Longhill +5.5 Attainment 8 points for all pupils and +6.2 for disadvantaged pupils; Brighton Aldridge +5.1 and +6.7; Hove Park +4.6 and +5.3.
  • Being an exceptional school buys back only about half of what high absence costs. Nationally, moving an average school’s absence to where the highest 5% of schools sit costs about 4.5 Attainment 8 points; being a top-decile school on value added gains about 2.3 back (home).

The national picture, for context

  • England’s autumn-and-spring absence rose from 5.2% of sessions in 2018/19 to 8.7% in 2022/23 and has eased only to 8.0% in 2025/26. Illness has come most of the way back; unauthorised and other authorised absence have not. Severe absence has risen from 1.2% of pupils to 3.6% and is still near its peak (how absence changes).
  • Absence is sticky. Once the national swing is removed, 66% of the variation in overall absence is a school’s lasting level. Of the fifth of schools with the highest unauthorised absence before the pandemic, 62% are still in the top fifth now. But more than a third are not: it moves.

5. Disadvantaged children here travel furthest — almost uniquely in England

  • Across England, children in deprived neighbourhoods usually live closer to a secondary school. That holds in cities, towns and the countryside, and in 81% of the 293 districts.
  • Brighton and Hove is the other way round. Children in the most deprived third of its neighbourhoods live 1.57 km from their nearest school; the rest live 1.18 km. That is 33% further — the 5th largest difference of 293 districts proportionately and the 2nd largest in kilometres (distance to school).
  • Among the 109 mostly urban local authorities, none has its deprived neighbourhoods further from a school.

On real streets and bus timetables

  • By walking and bus, children in the city’s most deprived neighbourhoods are 4.7 minutes further from a school than the rest, and 9.0 minutes further walking. No comparator place is more than 1.3 minutes further, and among all 109 mostly urban authorities the next largest gaps are Tameside (3.7) and Derby (3.4).
  • 18% of those journeys need two or more buses — the most of any authority in England.
  • From Whitehawk, the typical journey is two buses across the city: about 39 minutes to the quickest school and 44 to Dorothy Stringer, both changing in the city centre. In Bristol, Southampton and Plymouth a typical deprived neighbourhood’s journey is a walk or one short bus ride, 15 to 20 minutes.
  • 29 of the city’s 179 neighbourhoods — about 385 cohort-aged children — can reach no secondary school place at all within 30 minutes by walking and bus on a weekday morning. Not their catchment school; any school.
  • Access and child poverty line up, and in the wrong direction: they correlate at -0.33, and 25 neighbourhoods (about 400 children) sit in the least accessible third and the most deprived third at once — against only 11 in the most deprived third and the best-served third (the school system model, §4).

It traces to 2005

  • The city allocates by catchment with a lottery tie-break — very unusual in England — and that arrangement dates from the closure of CoMArt in the east of the city in 2005 and the catchment system that replaced distance-based allocation in 2008. The shape of the system is still partly the shape of that closure.
  • A school back on CoMArt’s old site would bring the deprived third to 1.19 km and close most of the gap (the city would fall from 5th to 27th of 293 districts). Longhill moved to the top of Elm Grove does much the same: 1.21 km, 37th. On the city’s school system model, CoMArt reopened would cut disadvantaged children’s journeys by 2.1 minutes.

How firm the link with absence is

Stated carefully, because the number is easy to over-read.

  • Across 150 authorities and six years from 2018/19, disadvantaged pupils’ absence is 3.5 points higher for each extra kilometre between deprived neighbourhoods and a school, against 0.6 for non-disadvantaged pupils. It holds on eleven measures of distance and is far weaker in primary schools, as it should be if the journey to secondary school is what matters.
  • But most of that slope is who lives in those places, not the journey. Given the ethnic mix of disadvantaged pupils in each authority it falls to 0.6, and with every control to 0.3, with an interval including zero.
  • A smaller link survives three separate tests. In mostly urban authorities outside London, 2.5 points per kilometre given ethnic mix and region, against 0.4 in primary. Within one authority, 0.45 points per kilometre for pupils in the most deprived neighbourhoods. And each doubling of how far a school’s nearest children live goes with 0.26 points more absence in every year. Across the 109 routed authorities, 1.9 points per kilometre with every control (2.5 outside London).
  • Carried to GCSE results, it is an “if”. If the extra absence is caused by the journey, each kilometre would cost disadvantaged pupils about 2.2 to 2.7 Attainment 8 points, and the city’s gap about 1 point. Attainment 8 modelled directly shows no such effect, so this is a plausible upper bound, not a measured one.

6. The demographic crisis is locked in

The children who will enter Year 7 in 2033 are already in the city’s primary schools. Their number is a count, not a forecast.

  • Reception is already down 20.4% since 2018/19, primary-age pupils down 13.7%, and the city is classed as in consistent decline (pupil numbers and school places).
  • Year 7 falls from 2,284 now to about 1,825 in 2032/33 — a fall of 20%, the 9th steepest of 159 areas in England. The secondary roll falls 10.4% by 2029/30.
  • On the school system model, that implies something like 765 surplus Year 7 places by 2033 — two and a half average secondary schools’ worth — and 93% of the city’s empty Year 7 places currently sit in just three schools. That is the difference between a budgeting problem and a viability problem.

  • The roll has fallen from 908 to 723 in four years, putting it in the bottom sixth of English secondaries by size. Per-pupil income rose — the funding system working as intended — and per-pupil spending rose faster.
  • At the recent rate of depletion, about £143,000 a year, the reserve supports roughly 3.7 more years. That makes this a decision with a deadline rather than one that can be deferred to a consultation cycle.
  • Longhill also carries the city’s highest absence (16.8%), highest persistent absence (48%), lowest value added (-1.5), highest FSM (40.1%) and a June 2026 report card of Needs attention on achievement and on curriculum and teaching. Its roll and its absence are the same story told twice.

But the money is not only a Longhill problem

  • 45% of the city’s 47 maintained schools were in deficit in 2025-26, against 21% across England and 17% in the South East — 9th highest of 129 authorities. The peak was 58% in 2024-25, the highest of 129 authorities that year (Brighton and Hove: school finances).
  • Together they hold a net deficit of £3.0m, against net reserves of £7.8m in 2021-22. The typical maintained school’s reserve is 0.2% of a year’s income against England’s 5.6%.
  • Eight open schools sit in the early-warning band and five show three or more warning signs — the most of any Sussex district.
  • Nine of the ten secondaries have been spending reserves, and the two deepest deficits are not Longhill — they are Hove Park and Cardinal Newman, one of them the largest school in the city.

The national pattern this sits in

  • England’s primary-age pupils peaked in 2018/19 and are 5.3% down by 2025/26; Year 7 will be about 10% smaller in 2032/33, and 15% or more smaller in 28 areas.
  • Rolls fall first and money follows. Of the 241 primary schools that closed or merged from 2017/18, the typical one had lost 23% of its pupils by the year before closing and was 63% full against 93% for schools that stayed open. Its reserves only slipped in the last two or three years: by the time the deficit shows, the pupils have gone (school finances before closure).
  • A simple early-warning model on published returns picks this up: trained on closures to 2020/21, the riskiest 5% of returns contained 69% of the schools that closed in the test years (AUC 0.92). Falling rolls carry most of the warning.
  • Closures have followed falling rolls in inner London and almost nowhere else. Most falling areas carry surplus places instead. Outside London, Brighton and Hove is among the areas that have closed most for their size.

7. It is fertility — not families leaving, and not house prices

  • The city’s total fertility rate is 0.98 children per woman (2025) against England’s 1.39 — about 70% of the national rate.
  • On the steadier three-year average (2023–25) it is 0.97, the joint second-lowest of 292 comparable districts, level with Westminster and behind only Cambridge (0.92). Worth quoting on this basis rather than on one year: a single year moves, and the three-year figure is what the analysis ranks. On 2025 alone the city is 5th of all 318 districts, behind Cambridge, Islington, Westminster and the City of London.
  • Births fell from 2,952 in 2015 to about 2,100 a year since 2022.

Births, not migration

Splitting the change in pupils exactly into births, migration since birth, and the share of children in state mainstream schools:

  • Primary pupils fell 14% from 2018/19 to 2025/26, and births account for all of it (-18.6 on the log scale ×100). Migration (+2.5) and the state-school share (+1.4) slightly offset it.
  • The city does lose children — about 92% of each birth cohort is still living there at primary age, against England’s 105% — but that loss is long-standing. It explains the low level, not the fall.
  • For comparison, in the median London borough primary pupils fell 12.1, split roughly half births and half families leaving. Brighton and Hove is not that story.

Where the city sits on child migration

  • It is in the losing minority, and near the wrong end of it. Net migration of children aged 0–10 runs at -4.7 per 1,000 a year (2019–25): 31st of 297 districts. Only 45 of the 297 lose children at all, and the median district gains 14 per 1,000.
  • It is the UK, not abroad. Within the UK the city loses 12.1 per 1,000; from abroad it gains 7.4, which cancels more than half of it. Adults aged 30–39 behave the same way: -14.6 internal, +11.9 international.
  • The neighbours gain children. Lewes gains 19.6 children per 1,000 a year, Mid Sussex 25.5 and Wealden 33.5 — districts with much the same house prices and fertility half again as high (the fertility model). These are each district’s net figures, not flows between them: they do not show where the city’s own families go.
  • But the loss is mild by city standards and it is easing. Lambeth loses 43 per 1,000, Wandsworth 42, Hackney 36. And the city’s own net loss has roughly halved, from -12.9 in 2013–18 to -4.7 in 2019–25. A loss that is shrinking cannot be what made the rolls fall.
  • And it did not turn up when the admissions changes came in. The obvious suspicion — that the city’s handling of admissions from 2023 pushed families out before secondary school — does not survive the test: the cohorts entering Year 7 under the new map sit fractionally above the trend that was already running, and child out-migration has improved. The tests and their limits are in section 8.
NoteOne qualification, and it matters for secondary schools

The “births, not migration” split holds for primary, and for the city’s shortage of children overall. It does not hold for Year 7. On the same decomposition, the 2018 to 2025 fall in Year 7 was -7.1 in total: births -4.4 and migration -5.1, so migration was the larger half, and the city ranks 33rd of 148 areas on that term (the median area is +2.1). The reason is retention: the share of a birth cohort still living in the city at age 11 fell from 92% to 87%.

So the honest division is: the city has few children because of fertility, and its secondary rolls are falling for both reasons about equally. For primary planning, births are the story. For secondary planning — which is what the rest of this page is about — families leaving before Year 7 is doing as much work as the birth rate, and it is the half a council could in principle influence.

House prices explain little

  • Across districts, the house price to earnings ratio accounts for 7% of the differences in fertility. Women’s education alone accounts for 27%. A five-measure model — women’s education, the share of women in their early twenties, partnership, private renting and birthplace — reaches 67%.
  • The city sits 0.44 children per woman below what its house-price ratio alone predicts, the 2nd largest gap in England. Add the other four measures and the gap disappears entirely (residual +0.01).
  • Brighton and Hove’s gap with the average district, by measure: a young, largely student population -0.13; highly educated women -0.13; few married or civil partnered couples -0.14 (274th of 288); private renting -0.10; house prices themselves -0.06. Housing, through renting and prices together, is about a third of the gap.
  • The neighbours are the test. Lewes, Mid Sussex and Wealden have house price to earnings ratios of 10.1 to 10.7 against the city’s 10.6 to 11.1 — and fertility of 1.45 to 1.58.
  • The fall itself is national. The city fell 0.41 children per woman; the typical English district fell 0.39. Where housing became less affordable, fertility fell no more than where it did not. Nothing local explains the fall in any district: within districts over time, local changes explain about half of one per cent of the movement.
  • One genuinely unusual feature. In 97% of districts most of the fall came from mothers under 30, which is consistent with births being postponed. In Brighton and Hove, Cambridge and Oxford it splits evenly — -0.21 under 30 and -0.20 at 30 and over. In these cities later births have not made up for fewer early ones.
  • Within the city the shortfall is central. Brunswick and the North Laine have 47 under-fives per 1,000 adults aged 20 to 44, against the city’s 122 and England’s 172; Mile Oak, Hangleton North and Portslade by Sea are at or above England’s. 22% of the city’s output areas have fewer than 60, against 5% across England. Neighbourhood make-up accounts for almost all of it.
  • But do not read that map as a list of schools to shrink. Across England and within the city, primaries in low-child areas have not lost Reception pupils faster than others. The council’s own forecasts leave the most unfilled places in the outer planning areas — The Deans 63%, West Blatchington and North Hangleton 59%, Portslade 43% — which have plenty of young children (note for the council).

8. Social mixing is the weakest lever available

This section is the one that most directly contradicts recent policy, so it is set out with the arithmetic.

How much a school can reach at all

  • Sixty years of school-effectiveness research puts the share of variation in outcomes attributable to schools rather than to their intakes at 8 to 15 per cent. Decomposing the multilevel model lands in the same place: a school can reach about 12% of the variance in its results, against roughly 42% that arrives with the intake.
  • Almost all of that reachable 12% runs through attendance. The workforce a school hires accounts for about 2.5% of the variance for all pupils and 0.8% for disadvantaged pupils; the school-controllable half of absence accounts for 9% and 11.7% respectively — fourteen times larger for the group the policy is aimed at.
  • Moving children between schools touches neither term. It changes which building a child attends without changing their attendance or their circumstances.
  • Nationally, absence explains 56% of the variation in school-level Attainment 8 and prior attainment 50%, while deprivation explains 21%.

Disadvantaged pupils do not do worse where they are concentrated

  • Given their prior attainment, disadvantaged pupils do slightly better where more of their schoolmates are disadvantaged — an elasticity of +0.027 (t = 7.0) in their own model. The effect is small, and it appears to run through schools organised to serve those pupils rather than through concentration itself.
  • Disadvantaged pupils’ value added does not rise with the school’s neighbourhood education. Across England the two correlate at 0.12, but that is London; outside London it is -0.05, and within local authorities -0.02.
  • Schools in more educated neighbourhoods have wider gaps, not narrower. Outside London the gap between non-disadvantaged and disadvantaged pupils rises from 10.9 points in the least educated fifth of neighbourhoods to 14.9 in the most. Each standard deviation of neighbourhood education widens it by 1.0 point (t = 11).
  • A standard deviation of neighbourhood education is worth +0.8% of Attainment 8 to a disadvantaged pupil, against +2.6% to a non-disadvantaged one. The thing being redistributed is worth little to the children it is being redistributed for.
  • And the city’s schools are already relatively mixed: 47th least segregated of 152 authorities on Gorard’s index in 2024/25 (0.129 against a median of 0.145). Adding local segregation to the value added model changes the city’s authority effect not at all (+3.7% against +3.7%).

BACA is the local proof

Brighton Aldridge Community Academy has the most disadvantaged intake in the city (FSM 52.6%), the city’s highest absence after Longhill, and about 30% of pupils arriving with low prior attainment.

  • Value added +2.2 points for all pupils, +2.4 for disadvantaged pupils and +2.6 for non-disadvantaged — above expectation for both groups, with a gap of only +0.2. On the earlier panel model a disadvantaged pupil leaving BACA in 2024/25 did about 5.7 Attainment 8 points better than a comparable pupil at a similar school elsewhere in England, and 3.1 points better averaged over four years.
  • Ofsted rated it Good in every area in April 2025.
  • That is far larger than the fraction of a point any redistribution of pupils could deliver.
  • Portslade Aldridge tells a similar story: +3.1 points overall, the joint strongest in the city.

What the city’s admissions changes actually did

On the city’s own school system model, run on its published settings:

  • The 2024 catchment map lengthened disadvantaged children’s journeys by about 0.3 minutes, and priority 6 by another 0.06 — against a 4.7-minute gap that comes from where the schools are, not from the rules.
  • Mixing moved by 0.011 on the model’s index, about 9% of the segregation before it — and the model itself warns that differences of a few thousandths are inside what a change of measure has moved.
  • In Attainment 8 points, that is a journey cost of about 0.06 against a generous mixing gain of up to 0.20. Both are a fraction of a point.

But the city average hides Whitehawk.

  • The six zones moved out of Longhill’s catchment and into Dorothy Stringer’s and Varndean’s are in Whitehawk and the edge of Roedean and Marina: about 78 children a year, 59 of them disadvantaged, in neighbourhoods with an IDACI score of 0.43 against 0.14 for the zones the map left alone.
  • Their journeys, already among the longest in the city, lengthened by 3.1 minutes on average to 57, and by 4.5 minutes to 56 for the disadvantaged children among them. The share offered Longhill fell from 22% to 5%.
  • On the chain, that is up to about 0.7 Attainment 8 points each — ten times the city average, for the city’s poorest children.
  • Putting Whitehawk back would shorten disadvantaged children’s journeys by 0.5 minutes and raise the segregation index by 0.015.

The proposals that did not go ahead would have been worse

The council first proposed priority 6 at 20% with Dorothy Stringer and Blatchington Mill each cut from 330 places to 300. Class Divide argued for 30% with the same cuts. Neither had a published model run, so both were run here on the published model.

Did the admissions changes drive families out of the city? No sign of it

A reasonable suspicion, and one worth testing rather than asserting: if the city’s handling of admissions from 2023 put families off, more of them should have left before secondary school. Tested two ways, it did not happen.

Test 1: the primary-to-secondary step. Year 7 pupils as a share of the same cohort’s Year 6 the year before — the most direct measure available of children not continuing into a city secondary.

Fitting the pre-policy trend in the Brighton-and-England gap on entry years to 2023, and asking whether the post-map cohorts sit below it:

  • the gap was already widening at -0.225 points a year before the change (standard error 0.134, p = 0.145 — not significant on its own);
  • scatter about that trend is 0.87 points;
  • entry 2024 lands +0.26 points above the trend (0.29 of a residual standard deviation), and entry 2025 +0.05 above (0.06).

Both post-map cohorts are fractionally better than the existing trend predicted — the opposite sign to the suspicion, and far too small to read either way. 2022 was already as low as 2024. The slide begins around 2016 and simply continues.

Test 2: child migration, year by year. Internal (within-UK) net migration of children aged 0–10 went from -18.8 per 1,000 in 2015–19 to -10.9 in 2023–25, an improvement of 7.9. Every year from 2020 to 2025 was better than the pre-pandemic norm, and 2024 — the first year of the new map — had the lowest outflow in the fourteen-year series. The city sits at the 88th percentile of 297 districts for that improvement, against a median district that barely moved.

ImportantWhat this null result is, and is not

It is not an exoneration, and it is not evidence the policy was good. It is evidence that one specific charge — that the changes pushed families out of the city — does not show up in the data that exists.

Four limits, stated so the test is not oversold:

  • Only two post-policy cohorts exist. Against 0.87 points of scatter, an effect of about a point would be invisible. This is a weak test.
  • The measure is confounded. A Year 6 pupil missing from Year 7 may have moved house, gone independent, been home educated, moved to a special school, or crossed into East Sussex or to Peacehaven. Nothing here separates them; the council’s admissions records would (section 10, release 3).
  • The 10 and 11 year olds cannot be isolated. The migration series is a 0–10 aggregate, so it is dominated by younger children; the age-specific version needs ONS components of change by single year of age.
  • The real test is 2026 entry, published June 2027: the first cohort applying with priority 6 live.

What is true, and predates all of this: the city retains far fewer of its own Year 6 cohort than England does — 90 to 94% against 98%, so roughly one in ten does not appear in a city Year 7 — and that gap has widened by about two points over a decade. That is a real and longstanding leak. It is not a post-2023 one.

NoteWhat this does not say

It does not say schools do not matter: a persistent school effect of 15.6% across 3,500 secondaries is a great deal, and the city’s schools mostly perform above what their intakes predict. Nor does it say segregation between schools is acceptable — there are fairness arguments for mixed intakes that have nothing to do with attainment. The claim is narrower and harder to argue with: the council should not expect a redistribution policy to move attainment much, because the mechanism it works through is the smallest term available. And this is school-level evidence, which cannot isolate what one pupil would gain by moving.

9. Four things that would help

In rough order of how much they would move, per pound and per year of political capital spent. The first is free; the second is the largest lever on results.

1. Publish a value-added measure, and point families at it

The cheapest large change available: no money, no consultation, and it could be in the next admissions guide. The evidence is in section 3; the action is three things.

  • Change the sentence in the admissions guide. It currently sends parents to each school’s exam results and Ofsted reports — the numbers that are four-fifths intake.
  • Print a contextualised measure beside Attainment 8 wherever the council publishes school results. One exists for every state secondary in England, on this site, built from published data and free to reuse.
  • Use it in the council’s own papers. Consultation documents, scrutiny reports and the case made for or against any individual school currently lean on the headline figure, which is the number least able to bear the weight.

On the city’s model this is the largest single change modelled anywhere in this work: Brighton Aldridge from 90 children to 174 on 180 places, the concentration of empty Year 7 places in three schools falling from 93% to 75%, and deprived children’s journeys shortening — without creating or removing a single place.

It will not raise anyone’s results by itself. It stops the city actively misdirecting families, and it stops the self-fulfilling loop that empties the schools doing most for its poorest children.

2. Attendance, treated as the city’s first-order problem

The largest lever in the model and the only large one that narrows the gap: 3.5% of Attainment 8 per 10% cut in absence for disadvantaged pupils, against 1.9% for others. About half of a school’s absence is structural and about half is not. The city’s extra absence is mostly authorised illness, which is where the enquiry should start — including the question of how it is recorded.

The published tables that would sharpen this are listed on the geographical effects page: absence by pupil characteristics by authority (is the city’s extra absence among FSM pupils or not, and in which year groups?), persistent absence and reasons over time, elective home education and children missing education, and attainment by prior-attainment band.

3. A school children in the east can actually get to

A smaller Longhill at the top of Elm Grove, with the catchments redrawn around it, is the option that does most for access and most for Longhill’s roll:

  • neighbourhoods reaching no place within 30 minutes falls from 29 to 15 — 145 children gain a reachable school place;
  • places reachable within half an hour rises from 452 to 482 for the average child, and the journey to the nearest school falls by a minute;
  • the gap between deprived children’s journeys and everyone else’s falls from 3.9 minutes to 0.4;
  • Whitehawk’s disadvantaged children’s journeys fall from 57 minutes to 23;
  • Longhill takes 106 children in 2026 and 86 in 2030 at 150 places, against 89 and 70 today;
  • 153 of 179 neighbourhoods are better off and 26 — the far south east — are worse.

It raises the segregation index, from 0.111 to 0.151. That counts against it under the council’s stated aim of evening out intakes. On the evidence about disadvantaged pupils’ outcomes in section 8, it is not a cost to those pupils. A review of how to even out disadvantaged children’s chances has to choose between those two measures, and should do so knowing both halves. It also does not make Longhill break even — about £0.7m a year at Elm Grove in 2030, £0.9m at Ovingdean — and that gap should go to members as the price of keeping a school in the east.

4. Treat the bus network as an admissions instrument

The only lever here that widens real choice without taking anything from anyone. 29 neighbourhoods can reach no place in half an hour, concentrated in the more deprived third of the city. A bus timetable can change that; a catchment boundary cannot.

One worked case: a child near a stop on the Whitehawk-to-Longhill school buses (the 72 and 72A, three trips each way) who misses the last workable journey arrives about 17 minutes late, against 0 to a few minutes at the other city schools; walking would take about 70 minutes. Longhill also has the city’s highest absence. Across England this is a striking case, not a pattern — the correlation between the penalty and absence is near zero nationally — so it is a reason to look at Longhill, not a general law.

10. The evidence, and the gap in it

What has been published, and when

Everything above comes from published data. The analysis behind it has been public since How to Pull the Right Lever, and the city’s school system model, its simulator and this site are all open, with code.

  • Absence was not part of the policy conversation. It was not mentioned in the public engagement slides or the published materials for the 2024 admissions consultation, nor for the 2025 one — in a city with the worst or second worst absence in England and near-average disadvantage.
  • The free school meals priority cannot yet be reviewed on outcomes, in either direction. It took effect for entry in September 2023. Those children sit GCSEs in summer 2028. Not one cohort admitted under the policy has reached an examination.
  • And a single year of disadvantaged results is not evidence about a school. For disadvantaged pupils, year-to-year noise (3.6 Attainment 8 points) is larger than the persistent difference between schools (2.7 points). Two schools doing genuinely equally well by their disadvantaged pupils will differ by around 5 points in a given year for no reason at all — larger than most of the gaps that get argued about. Three years averaged, or it is mostly noise.

Ten tables that would settle most of what is open

None requires new collection. All are aggregates the council already holds. The full list is in the school system model (§10.3); the first five:

  1. home LSOA of every applicant, by year and allocated school;
  2. the full preference ordering, anonymised, by year;
  3. allocated school by home LSOA, including schools outside the city;
  4. the criterion under which each place was allocated;
  5. children allocated a school they did not name, by school, year and home catchment.

One of these has in effect already happened. Appendix 1 of the council’s Educational Equity Framework counts Key Stage 2 and Key Stage 4 pupils by ward and MSOA, as a by-product of describing attainment. Those counts gave the model’s origins their first external check: the geography holds, correlating at 0.96. But the council’s own place planning applies a single state-sector share of 0.85 city-wide, where the real range runs from about 0.66 in Round Hill to 1.21 in Woodingdean — enough to change which schools look full. A table published for another purpose answered a question that had been described as unanswerable.

What would change these conclusions

  • The firmest findings are the demographic ones: they are counts of children already in school, adjusted by a transfer rate that has moved under two percentage points in a decade.
  • The accessibility findings are firm in direction and approximate in magnitude: modelled journey times, not measured ones.
  • The value added and absence findings are associations between schools, not effects on pupils. The absence gradient says what schools with more absence look like once intake is accounted for, not what one school would gain by cutting absence by a given amount. Direction and order of magnitude are what to take from them.
  • The school system model’s results are the softest: swept rather than calibrated, because calibration needs the tables above. Every comparison is robust across the swept range; no single number is a prediction.
