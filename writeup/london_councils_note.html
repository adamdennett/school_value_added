London Councils on the estate and on closures

London Councils' Managing school rolls and maintaining educational standards in London (January 2026) is mostly about funding and powers. It also sets out how boroughs plan the estate and decide closures, and the boroughs' forecasts behind it probably understate London's fall by about half.

Planning the estate

  • Keep closed sites. Recommendation 5: boroughs need more decision-making powers, capital funding to adapt sites, and the freedom to retain and manage closed school buildings locally.
    • Births may recover, and London needs about 880,000 new homes over the next decade.
    • Without protecting sites, growth could take land later needed for schools.
  • Reuse surplus space before closing. Reduce published admission numbers; add resourced provision for pupils with SEN, which uses spare capacity and brings extra funding; open nurseries (School-Based Nursery Capital Grant) and family hubs. Some boroughs have turned primaries due to close into special schools.
  • Plan across borough boundaries. Recommendation 4: secondary pupils travel further, so one borough's change puts pressure on its neighbours. DfE's London team should plan new schools, including special schools, across boroughs.
  • The barrier is academies. Boroughs cannot take a strategic view across all their schools, and some academies do not engage. The report welcomes the new co-operation duties in the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act but thinks the wording too weak to enforce (Recommendation 3).

Planning for closures

  • Boroughs must close schools that have become financially unviable, especially where no rise in children is forecast, because keeping them open harms the local system.
  • Decisions weigh parental preference, Ofsted ratings, travel routes, projected demand and financial sustainability.
  • Boroughs say they work to avoid disadvantaging particular groups. Finding 5 shows closures nationally still fall on schools with more disadvantaged pupils and more pupils with SEN, outside London too.
  • One-form-entry primaries are the most financially vulnerable.

  • The boroughs' 2025/26 Reception forecast was 87,415; the census found 84,679.
  • Measured from where pupil numbers actually stand, London's fall is roughly twice what the report shows. That strengthens its own case for planning closures and reuse.
  • Other figures: £45m less funding over four years (£15m primary, £30m secondary); just over a quarter of maintained schools in deficit in 2024/25 (our CFR figure for London maintained primaries is 28%); 17% of the 2012/13 Reception cohort had left London by Year 6, and 20% of the 2017/18 cohort.
  • Caution: the report gives about 90 closures or mergers in 2019 to 2025 in one footnote and about 40 in another. Cite carefully, or use the GIAS count.

What it does not have: any method for choosing which schools to close, reshape or reuse. There is no analysis of capacity or sites, no modelling of journeys, and no test of who is affected. The simulator exemplar puts numbers on each of the five factors for every school.
