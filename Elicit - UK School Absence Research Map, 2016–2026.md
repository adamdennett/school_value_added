# School absence in the UK: research map, 2016–2026

## Main picture

Over the last decade, UK evidence has increasingly treated school absence not as one behaviour with one cause, but as an outcome of interacting health, family, peer, school and structural conditions. It is consistently more common in secondary than primary school, rises through adolescence, and is concentrated among pupils facing poverty, special educational needs and disabilities (SEND), health difficulties and other forms of disadvantage. Attendance and attainment are strongly related, but absence is both a mechanism through which disadvantage can affect learning and a marker of prior or concurrent difficulties; the evidence does not support treating every absence as voluntary truancy or assuming a single causal pathway. [^1][^2][^3][^4][^5][^6]

The post-COVID rise is real, large, and not yet fully explained. In England, absence has improved from its post-pandemic peak, but remained above 2018/19 levels in the latest accredited data located for this review (2024/25). Persistent absence fell in that period, while severe absence continued to rise. [^3] Research offers plausible interacting explanations—illness and health, anxiety and school avoidance, disrupted routines and weakened attendance norms, unmet SEND needs, family-school friction, and barriers to timely support—but no single explanation has been established as the dominant cause nationally. [^7][^8]

## Scope and how to use this map

This selective research map is not a formal systematic review or complete bibliography. It prioritises England and includes Scotland and Wales where relevant.

“Absence” includes illness and other authorised absence as well as unauthorised absence; persistent absence is a threshold, not a diagnosis or explanation. In England, persistent absence means missing at least 10% of possible sessions, while severe absence means missing at least 50%. [^3]

## Scale and distribution: a post-pandemic step change, with mixed recent movement

In England, accredited Department for Education statistics for autumn and spring 2024/25 put overall absence at 6.63%, down from 6.93% in 2023/24, but still above the pre-pandemic level, which had been consistently below 5%. Persistent absence was 17.63%, down from 19.23% the previous year but well above 10.53% in 2018/19. By contrast, severe absence rose to 2.26% (166,000 pupils), from 2.14% in 2023/24 and 0.81% in 2018/19. [^3]

The burden is higher in secondary schools, though primary absence is also substantial. In 2024/25, overall absence was 8.09% in secondary schools versus 5.16% in primary schools; persistent absence was 21.86% versus 13.31%. Special schools had still higher rates. Secondary absence also increases with age: the highest overall and severe absence rates were in Year 11, while persistent absence peaked in Year 10. In primary, Year 1 and younger pupils stand out as a concern: their overall rate did not fall in the latest combined-term comparison, unlike most other year groups. [^3]

The trend is not simply “absence keeps rising.” EPI’s analysis of autumn 2024/25 found overall and persistent absence falling, especially in secondary schools, and the first post-pandemic fall in “unauthorised other” absence; yet severe absence remained elevated and had not clearly turned down. The direction therefore depends on which measure, age group and pupil group is examined. A short-term improvement in the average can coexist with a worsening tail of pupils missing very large amounts of schooling. [^9]

## Factors associated with absence

### Health, mental health and school-related distress

Physical illness is the most frequently recorded reason for absence in England. In 2024/25 illness accounted for 3.38% of possible sessions, lower than the previous year but still above the pre-pandemic trend of below 3%. [^3]

Mental health and attendance are linked in both directions. Anxiety, depression, behavioural and emotional difficulties can make attending harder; prolonged absence can also reduce social connection and access to supportive routines, and may contribute to later difficulties. A 2026 England cohort study using linked administrative and survey data found that persistent absence at ages 14–15 was associated with higher odds of self-reported mental-health problems at 16–17, even after adjustment; the association strengthened with longer durations of absence. The authors appropriately describe a longitudinal association, not proof that absence itself caused the later problems. [^6]

Qualitative work also cautions against reducing distress-related absence to “school refusal” or blaming families. In a study of parents and professionals supporting primary-aged pupils during COVID-era reopening, attendance difficulties were especially prominent among children with SEND and pre-existing anxiety. Participants described COVID-related fear, difficulty readjusting to routines, academic catch-up pressure, reduced access to specialist provision and strained home-school communication as interacting pressures. [^7]

### SEND, disability and health needs

SEND is among the clearest and most persistent correlates. A longitudinal analysis of 418,455 mainstream secondary pupils in England found higher absence among pupils with SEN after adjustment for measured socioeconomic and demographic factors; physical disability was associated with the highest rates, followed by behavioural, emotional and social difficulties. The relationship varied substantially across local authorities, suggesting that pupil composition alone may not explain all local differences, although the study cannot identify which policies account for them. [^2]

Recent English statistics show that SEND gaps have not closed with the overall recovery. In 2024/25, overall absence for pupils with SEN was just under twice that of pupils without identified SEN; absence among pupils with an education, health and care plan increased, and the severe-absence gap widened. EPI notes that administrative SEND categories capture identified needs only, leaving some unmet or unidentified need outside the statistics. [^3][^9]

### Poverty, family circumstances and place

Socioeconomic disadvantage is strongly patterned with absence. In England in 2024/25, pupils eligible for free school meals had nearly twice the overall absence rate and more than two-and-a-half times the persistent-absence rate of their non-eligible peers; their severe-absence likelihood was over three-and-a-half times as high. Scottish research similarly links neighbourhood income differences with secondary-school absence and highlights plausible pathways through health, housing, family resources and school mobility. [^3][^10]

The relationship is not reducible to parental attitudes. Family illness, caring responsibilities, conflict, housing insecurity, poverty and limited access to services can all constrain attendance; at the same time, supportive parental engagement and home-school relationships can protect against persistent absence. Qualitative evidence from secondary-school practitioners identifies individual, family, peer and school factors, while warning that staff may overemphasise home explanations and under-recognise school climate, relationships, bullying, curriculum fit, transitions and academic pressure. Because this was a small qualitative study in three schools, it maps perceptions and possible mechanisms rather than estimating prevalence or causal effects. [^1]

Absence also varies by ethnicity, sex and geography. The England 2024/25 release reports especially high rates among Gypsy/Roma and Irish Traveller pupils, and higher rates among girls than boys; the North East had the highest overall regional absence, while London had among the lowest. These figures describe differences in recorded absence, rather than establishing their causes. [^3]

## Effects on attainment and unequal consequences

The most direct pathway is missed instruction, but absence can also weaken participation, peer connection and continuity in learning. Evidence from linked England and Wales cohorts indicates that absence in any school year is associated with lower attainment by the end of compulsory schooling, with larger penalties in particular years: early primary, the final primary year, and several secondary years. The study also reports that authorised absence can be as damaging to attainment as unauthorised absence, underscoring that the learning opportunity missed—not the administrative label alone—matters. [^4]

Absence is also one pathway through which socioeconomic disadvantage can become an attainment gap. A 2026 England analysis of 8,024 children estimated that absence explained around 6.45–8.10% of the socioeconomic association with achievement in Year 2, with similar mediation patterns reported for later school years. This is evidence of a partial pathway, not that attendance alone can remove the gap. [^5]

For secondary pupils in Scotland, whole-population linked data show distinct groups with different combinations of attendance, exclusions, attainment, health and socioeconomic circumstances. The authors caution against assuming a simple linear rule that every pupil with lower attendance will have proportionally lower attainment; some high-attaining pupils had relatively different attendance and exclusion profiles. Overall, the evidence supports absence as an important risk to learning and a contributor to inequality, while also requiring pupil-level interpretation rather than a single deficit label. [^11]

At the severe-absence threshold, pupils miss at least half of possible sessions; England had 166,000 such pupils in autumn and spring 2024/25. [^3]

## What explains the post-COVID persistence?

The strongest direct UK qualitative evidence points to amplification and interaction, rather than a wholly new single cause. Families and professionals described pre-existing anxiety and attendance difficulties worsening after closures; COVID-related illness and fear; a preference for the safety and flexibility of home; challenges rebuilding routines and peer contact; academic catch-up stress; and reduced or disrupted specialist services. Poor communication and mistrust between home and school could make recovery harder, while families described delayed access to support. These findings are explanatory accounts from a selected qualitative sample, not estimates of how common each mechanism is nationally. [^7]

A separate international analysis using PISA data found a marked post-pandemic rise in self-reported teenage skipping in England and other English-speaking countries, but no convincing association between the length of school closures and the increase. For England, the reported rise was about 9 percentage points relative to the 2012–2018 average. The outcome is self-reported skipping over the previous two weeks—not the full administrative absence rate—and therefore should not be conflated with illness-related or authorised absence. The authors suggest weakened attendance norms as one possible interpretation, but do not test it as a causal mechanism. [^8]

Taken together, these findings make a simple “lockdown caused the whole rise” explanation unlikely: cross-country variation in closure length did not track increases in teenage skipping, while the UK qualitative work documents several pandemic-amplified factors. [^8][^7] The persistence may reflect interacting health and mental-health burdens, school avoidance and disengagement, changing family and school expectations, unmet SEND and pastoral needs, and barriers to early response. This is a plausible synthesis, not a settled causal decomposition; the evidence reviewed does not assign the relative contribution of each mechanism nationally.

## Intervention evidence and implications

Intervention evidence is weaker than evidence describing the problem. A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised trials found a small positive effect for parental-engagement interventions on days attended, with moderate certainty but substantial heterogeneity; mentoring’s pooled estimate was uncertain and low certainty. Most trials were conducted in the United States, limiting direct transfer to UK schools. [^12]

The UK qualitative studies support practical priorities—early identification, listening to pupils and families, rebuilding trusting school-home relationships, addressing bullying and school belonging, tailoring SEND provision, and coordinating education with health and community services. These should be understood as promising, context-sensitive directions, not as proof that any one intervention reliably reduces absence at scale. The broader evidence points toward matching support to the reason and pattern of absence, rather than relying on a uniform attendance campaign or punitive response alone. [^7][^1]

## Where the research is thin

- **National causal explanation after COVID:** There are useful official trend series and qualitative accounts, but little UK-wide longitudinal work that combines reasons for absence, pupil and family circumstances, school experience, service access and attainment to estimate the contribution of each mechanism.
- **Primary-age children:** Current data show that primary absence is lower than secondary absence but still elevated; focused qualitative and intervention evidence is comparatively sparse. The pandemic-era primary study is valuable but regionally recruited and not designed to represent all families. [^7]
- **Pupil voice and diversity:** The available COVID-era interview study recruited predominantly White British mothers in southern England, limiting what it can say about fathers, other ethnic groups and regional variation. More research should include children themselves, especially pupils with SEND, long-term illness, caring responsibilities or precarious housing. [^7]
- **Severe and persistent absence:** The rising severe-absence tail deserves separate study from occasional absence and broad persistent-absence thresholds. Reasons and return pathways are likely to differ, and administrative statistics alone cannot establish them.
- **Attainment estimates by group:** There is evidence of average and socioeconomic-linked attainment effects, but less evidence on how the effect varies by SEND type, ethnicity, prior attainment, absence reason, and primary-to-secondary transition.
- **Intervention transferability:** More adequately powered UK trials and implementation evaluations are needed; international evidence is heterogeneous and heavily weighted toward US settings. [^12]
- **Comparability:** Definitions and collection methods differ among UK nations and across survey and administrative sources. PISA self-reported skipping captures a narrower behaviour than official absence records, while recent attendance data may be provisional before registers are finalised. [^8][^3]

## Selected starting points

1. **Department for Education, _Pupil absence in schools in England_ (2024/25 autumn and spring terms):** baseline for prevalence, school phase, reasons and pupil-characteristic differences. [^3]
2. **Education Policy Institute, _Examining post-pandemic absences in England (5)_ (2025):** interprets change across absence severity and pupil groups. [^9]
3. **McDonald, Lester & Michelson (2022), _“She didn’t know how to go back”_:** multi-stakeholder study of primary-age attendance problems during COVID-era reopening. [^7]
4. **Finning et al. (2019), _Secondary school practitioners’ beliefs about risk factors for school attendance problems_:** ecological account of individual, family, peer and school-related factors. [^1]
5. **Lereya et al. (2022), _How does the association between special education need and absence vary over time and across special education need types?_:** large longitudinal England secondary cohort. [^2]
6. **Dräger, Klein & Sosu (2025), _Does the impact of pupil absences on achievement depend on their timing?_:** linked England/Wales evidence on timing and attainment. [^4]
7. **Anders et al. (2025), _The rise in teenagers skipping school across English-speaking countries following the COVID-19 pandemic_:** separates self-reported teenage truancy from broader absence and tests the school-closure-duration hypothesis. [^8]
8. **Strelitz et al. (2026), _Interventions to improve school attendance_:** current RCT review; useful for intervention evidence and its transferability limitations. [^12]

## Conclusion

The evidence links absence with age, poverty, SEND, health and school experience, as well as lower attainment and wider disadvantage. [^3][^4][^5] England’s latest sourced statistics show overall and persistent absence improving but still above pre-COVID levels, while severe absence worsened. [^3] Post-COVID research identifies several potentially interacting explanations but does not quantify their relative importance. [^7][^8] The most consequential evidence gap is nationally representative longitudinal UK research linking absence reasons and trajectories to pupil needs, school conditions, services and attainment—especially for severely absent pupils and those with SEND.
